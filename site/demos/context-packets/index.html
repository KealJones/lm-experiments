Small model lab

Can a 350-word packet stand in for the whole conversation?

A small model can't hold a long, rambling chat, and big ones carry all of it all the time. So: one layer reads the chat, works out what "that party thing" or "the gas station dude" points to, and writes a short packet with only what the new message needs. Then the model answers from the packet alone.

It lost nothing that mattered. Opus answering from a packet built by Opus passed all 12 main tests, using about 7% of the words. Every trap survived the squeeze. Where it did break, the packet broke first: the answer was only as good as what the front layer wrote down.

    Where it broke: the packet first
    
  
    Both failures behind a Haiku packet trace straight back to the packet. The answering model
    trusts what it's handed, so the packet's slips become the answer's.
    
  
    
    
    
      
    Dinner party
    
      
    Opus's packet: No spicy ginger beer and the brother-in-law's dessert is cancelled.
        Haiku's packet put ginger beer on the drinks list and left the dessert open. With Tomas at the table, who
        can't do any spice, the reply offered ginger beer and brought in a man who isn't coming.
    
      
    
    
    
    
      
    "ok now divide that by 3"
    
      
    Opus's packet, 141 words: the 4 from "2+2", done. Haiku's packet: "that" most likely means the 4,
        followed by every eval score in the chat as other candidates. The reply answered 1.33, then hedged and
        divided 74.2 by 3 as well.
    
      
    
    
    
    
      
    A good packet didn't lift Haiku
    
      
    Haiku from an Opus packet passed 10 of 12, against 11 from the full history. Its misses were in the
        answering: a half-finished leaderboard, and not naming anything that happened on December 18. At this
        length, holding the context isn't what holds Haiku back.
    
      
    
    
  
    

    


    
  
    Junk in the packet
    
  
    Retrieval won't be clean, so six hand-written packets came stuffed with tangential and
    wrong material: the user's own birthday next to a web snippet with Emma Stone's, an outdated height, a forum
    post with the wrong year, and a question whose answer isn't there. Every model got all six right.

      What it says, and what it doesn't
      
  
        
    
      • A ~360-word packet carried everything these tests needed: the moved date, the replaced plumber quote, the
      constraint mentioned once as "that no-meat thing", the config detail from 90 turns back, the carpet the user
      liked but had sworn off.
        • 
    
      • The front layer is the weak link. A cheap builder writes a slightly wrong packet, and the strong answerer
      faithfully follows it. The builder needs to be as careful about what changed and what was ruled out as about
      what was said.
        • 
    
      • The chats are short, about 8,000 tokens, where the full history doesn't hurt a frontier model. So this
      shows a packet is enough, not that it's better. The real test is 50k+ tokens, or a 1B model
      with a 4k window.
        • 
    
      • It's near the ceiling, with 12 main tests, so one test either way is noise. Sonnet wrote the chats and Opus
      graded them. The builder read the whole chat, so this doesn't test finding the right bits cheaply.
        • 
  
      
  
      Run: Claude Code subagents, 6 Nov 2026, Opus, Sonnet and Haiku, about 150 agent runs, no GPU.
