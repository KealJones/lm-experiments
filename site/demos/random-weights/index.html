What does a language model say before it has learned anything?

Take GPT-2's shape, shrink it to 2 layers, and fill every weight with random numbers instead of training it. Try four ways of filling it, then the same in Qwen3's and Gemma 4's architectures.

Noise in the flavour of its tokenizer. None of the 12 random models beats guessing every token with equal odds. The bigger the random numbers, the more confidently wrong it gets.

      What it says

      • Nothing random beats a blind guess. The standard init comes closest, 0.009 above uniform odds in GPT-2. Its logits are so small that every token is nearly equally likely, which is the least wrong you can be.
      • Bigger random numbers make a confident idiot. At std 1 or 10 the logits blow up, and GPT-2 bets everything on a few tokens: 59 to 62 different top picks across 10,240 positions. It's wrong with huge confidence, so its loss lands far past the blind guess.
      • It barely reads the prompt. With near-flat logits, the random draw decides the output, not the input. Greedy decoding loops on one token within a few steps.
      • The tokenizer sets the flavour of the noise. GPT-2's gibberish is English-ish fragments. Qwen3's mixes Chinese, Korean and code. Gemma 4 pulls from dozens of scripts and <unused> tokens.
      • Everything a model "knows" comes from training. The architecture alone gives nothing measurable here.

      The live model runs in a Web Worker in plain JavaScript, with no libraries. Its random numbers come from this page's own generator, so a seed here is a new model of the same kind, not the exact weights the experiment drew in PyTorch. The tokenizer uses GPT-2's real vocabulary and merges, and gives the same token ids as Hugging Face's tokenizer on the test strings and the WikiText passage.
