# Shrink the haystack, externalize the hops

A tiny model's attention cannot be made to "just focus" by a new formula. The math sets a hard budget: with bounded logits, every softmax weight shrinks like 1/n as the number of competing tokens n grows. Sharp focus therefore needs a logit gap that grows roughly with log n, and chaining k lookups inside one forward pass needs about log₂k + 2 layers, or else one generated step per hop. Attention changes that respect this budget do help, but modestly and mostly when trained from scratch: gated attention, differential attention, log-n temperature, and sparse normalizers. When bolted onto a pretrained sub-1B model, they give small gains that depend on the benchmark or none at all. The two levers that move results at 350M–500M both belong to the model–harness pair. **The harness controls n** by handing over fewer, cleaner, well-labelled tokens. **The harness turns each hop into its own tool step**, which converts a hard latent composition into a sequence of easy single lookups. The model is then trained, first by imitation and then with per-hop rewards, to read what the harness returns and to keep calling until the goal is filled. Your pilot's pattern fits this exactly: 98–99% on trained task types, 26–28% on held-out combinations, and stopping after the town is found. That is most likely a policy and generalization failure, not an attention-geometry failure; Experiment 1 below checks this. The fix is harness enforcement plus combinatorially diverse training and RL, with attention retrofits as a secondary experiment. MLPs stay essential. At ~1–2 bits per parameter, a 350M model holds at most a few tens of megabytes of facts, so its MLP capacity is better spent on skills (formats, idioms, heuristics, reading and copying) than on encyclopedic recall. No ≤500 MB model is near expert TypeScript. A compiler-and-test loop can raise what a small model ships, but it cannot supply missing synthesis skill, so delegation to a larger model is part of an honest design.

## Softmax spreads attention at a rate of 1/n, and only the harness can cut n for free

The core result is simple and proven. If the attention logits stay inside a fixed range [m, M], then as the number of keys n grows every softmax weight is Θ(1/n). A transformer over a finite vocabulary with continuous layers keeps its logits bounded, so "attention coefficients must disperse in all global Transformer heads" on long enough inputs ([Veličković et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.01104)). The associative-memory view says the same thing from another angle. One attention step equals one update of a modern Hopfield network. Its fixed points are a global average, a metastable average over a subset, or a single stored pattern, and which one you get depends on how well the target key is separated from the rest relative to the inverse temperature ([Ramsauer et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.02217)). Put together, a head "focuses" when (logit gap × β) clearly exceeds about log n. The gap needs to be large, and n needs to be small.

Only three things can satisfy that inequality. The first is to make n smaller. The second is to scale logits with length, as in Scalable-Softmax, which multiplies the query by s·log n with one learnable scalar per head ([Nakanishi](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.19399)). The same idea appeared earlier as log-length scaling to escape softmax's theoretical limits ([Chiang & Cholak](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.12172)). The third is to replace softmax with something that can output exact zeros or "nothing". Normalizing on the sphere (QK-norm, cosine attention, nGPT) bounds the logit to ±scale. That helps training stability, but by the same lemma it guarantees dispersion unless the scale is learned or grows with length. **No theorem says any new geometry makes a model attend to "what is needed"**: such claims are unproven. Rank-collapse theorems about pure attention stacks ([Dong et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.03404)) do not describe a pre-LN model with residuals and MLPs. They are not the reason your model loses focus.

Small models live on the wrong side of this budget. They have small key dimensions, so fewer near-orthogonal directions are available to separate keys, and they have few heads. In models of 6B and up, under 5% of heads do the actual copy-from-context work, and pruning those specific heads breaks retrieval while pruning random heads does not ([Wu et al., retrieval heads](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.15574)). Nobody has published a retrieval-head map for a sub-1B model, including LFM2.5-350M or Qwen2.5-0.5B. The sub-1B evidence that does exist is sobering. In a controlled needle test, **Gemma 3 270M's middle-of-context accuracy collapses to 0% by 4K tokens**, while Qwen1.5-0.5B and Qwen2.5-0.5B keep 100% through 8K. Architecture matters even at the same size ([NeurIPS 2026 abstract](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2026/162683); verified from the abstract only). With natural rather than synthetic context the picture is worse. Given the **gold passage**, SmolLM2-360M answered 0% of questions it did not already know, and models from 360M to 7B ignored the passage in 61–100% of oracle failures ([Pandey, arXiv 2603.11513](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.11513)). Across 18 frontier and open models, a focused ~300-token prompt beat a ~113k-token prompt on LongMemEval for every model, and even one similar distractor hurt ([Chroma, Context Rot](https://www.trychroma.com/research/context-rot)).

Several modified attention mechanisms have been measured. The table below gives the verified results that matter at your scale.

The pattern is clear. **Mechanisms that let a head output nothing (gates) or cancel noise (differential) produce the most robust gains.** They show up mostly on long-context, multi-needle tests and in training stability. Retrofits onto pretrained sub-1B models range from small to negative. One side effect matters for a browser model. Removing attention sinks collapses massive activations: the gate takes max activation from 1053 to 94 ([Qiu et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.06708)), and softpick cuts kurtosis from 33,511 to 341 ([Softpick](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.20966)). That makes 4-bit quantization friendlier, which is a real benefit for a ≤500 MB model even when accuracy gains are small. Two harness-adjacent tools target attention directly. JuICE scales the outputs of about five identified heads and raised context-faithfulness averages to 66.2–66.8, against 51.9–60.0 for the best baseline, on ≥2B models ([JuICE](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.10996)). KBLaM gives the harness its own attention path: "rectangular attention" over knowledge tokens, linear in knowledge-base size ([KBLaM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.10450)). Neither has been tested below 1B.

## Chaining fails for lack of depth or a hop step, not for lack of geometry

The theory of multi-hop lookup is unusually crisp. A causally masked transformer can follow k pointer hops with **L = ⌊log₂k⌋ + 2 layers**, one head and constant width. Under a standard conjecture, sub-linear width needs Ω(log k) depth. A single layer needs either k chain-of-thought tokens or width that grows polynomially with input length ([Sanford, Hsu & Telgarsky, Thm 4.2, Cor 5.6](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.09268)). In real models the bridge entity resolves partway up the stack, and the second hop fails when too few layers remain. Back-patching later states into earlier layers fixes 32–66% of such failures in 7B–70B models. This is an oracle diagnostic, not a usable method ([Biran et al., Hopping Too Late](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12775)). Learning to compose implicitly is expensive. The data needed grows exponentially in the number of hops and the depth needed grows linearly, so 3-hop accuracy went 2.5% → 94.6% only between 1× and 10× data ([Yao et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.17923)). Grokked transformers compose in-distribution but **never** out of distribution unless lower and upper layers share weights ([Wang et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.15071)). The same layer-mismatch diagnosis, and the same looped fix, appear in [Zhang et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.07261).

Recurrent depth is the strongest architectural answer, and it has been retrofitted at your scale. Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct was split into prelude, weight-tied loop and coda. A **6M-parameter rank-16 LoRA** version reached 83.8% on a synthetic multi-step task, against 72.1% for a same-size scratchpad model, 27.7% for direct answering, and 36.6% for a direct-answering 1.5B model. Its zero-shot transfer to new phrasings was minimal (16–17%), learned depth selection failed, and training budgets were not matched across arms ([Shapiro, arXiv 2608.11233](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.11233)). Natively looped models also clearly beat dense ones on dependent tool calls: Ouro-2.6B scores 0.371 on NESTful versus 0.063 for SFT'd Qwen3-4B. Retrofitted 1B looped models gained less and were inconsistent ([Popescu et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.18171)).

Your agent does not need any of this to chain person → town → population, because **each hop is already a separate tool call**. That is the chain-of-thought regime, where depth is traded for tokens and short chains are easy. Inside its trained depth, the 0.5B scratchpad model matched latent recurrence ([2608.11233](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.11233)). Press et al. found that memorizing more facts does not shrink the compositionality gap, but asking explicit follow-up questions, especially with search, does ([Press et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.03350); gap sizes unverified). So when your model "finds the town and never looks up the population", the likely cause is not missing layers. There are two candidate failures. Either the model did not read the bridge entity out of the tool result, which is the utilization failure in [2603.11513](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.11513). Or it read the entity and its policy ended the episode, the classic behaviour of a model fit to a narrow set of episode shapes. A cheap probe tells these apart (Experiment 1 below), and they call for different fixes. The 98→27% drop from trained to held-out combinations reproduces in your own data what the recurrence retrofit showed on new surface forms: in-distribution skill does not transfer for free to unseen compositions. The literature's remedy is to make held-out structure part of training (more composed vs atomic examples, curricula, template diversity), not a cleverer attention kernel ([Wang et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.15071); [Behnia et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.16096)).

## A 350M model's MLP has room for skills, not encyclopedias

The MLP stays. Its role is clearer than "fact store". Feed-forward layers are about two-thirds of the parameters and behave as key→value memories over textual patterns ([Geva et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.14913)). They also hold computation: arithmetic runs on sparse sets of heuristic neurons, "a bag of heuristics" ([Nikankin et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.21272v1)). In pretrained LLMs they are far less removable than attention. Dropping 8 MLP layers took Mistral-7B from 70.3 to 53.4, whereas dropping 8 attention layers cost only 0.5 points ([He et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.15786v6)). The binding constraint is capacity. Language models store at most **~2 bits per parameter**, and only after ~1000 exposures per fact. At 100 exposures that falls to ~1 bit/param, junk-diluted data cuts useful capacity up to 20×, and int4 quantization cuts it by more than 2× ([Allen-Zhu & Li, Part 3.3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.05405)). A 350M model therefore holds on the order of 45–90 MB of facts at best, and a 4-bit browser build holds less. Facts are what a harness supplies cheaply, so the MLP's budget is better spent on things seen thousands of times: tool-call syntax, TypeScript idioms, reading-and-copying behaviour, and arithmetic or unit heuristics. Retrieval's measured wins also fall on knowledge, not skill. REALM-330M beat T5-11B on NaturalQuestions (40.4 vs 34.5) ([REALM](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2002.08909)), and RETRO matched GPT-3 on the Pile with 25× fewer parameters, but a RETRO reproduction was only "on par" with GPT on non-knowledge tasks ([Wang et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.06762)).

Better MLP math exists, but nearly all of it applies when training from scratch. SwiGLU raised a 125M model's zero-shot average from 42.6 to 43.9. Deep-and-thin beats wide-and-shallow at that size, and running each block twice (weight sharing) added +0.8 points at 350M ([MobileLLM](https://huggingface.co/papers/2402.14905.md)). That last result is a cheap way to buy depth, and so composition, without buying stored facts. Squared ReLU gives activation sparsity that on-device inference can exploit ([ReLU² Wins](https://huggingface.co/papers/2402.03804)). Gated MLPs store up to 1.3× fewer bits at low exposure, a small cost that matters less when facts come from tools ([Part 3.3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.05405)). Product-key memory layers are the strongest way to add factual capacity cheaply: at a 373M base, Memory+ beat a 5× larger MoE on TriviaQA (28.10 vs 19.94) ([Berges et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.09764v2)). The UltraMemV2 results add a warning, though: memory-layer models are **much worse at multi-hop reasoning (17.7 vs 34.8)** ([UltraMemV2](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.18756v1)). Chaining is exactly the skill your pair needs, so a big memory layer is the wrong trade for a harness-fed model. The MLP-side idea best matched to a harness-first design is to keep the dense MLP for skills and let retrieval own facts. Whether shrinking the MLP actually spares tool-use-with-supplied-facts while closed-book recall collapses has not been measured at any scale; Experiment 6 tests it.

## The pair wins when the harness owns facts, arithmetic and sequencing

Every documented case of "small model plus harness beats big model" comes from training the model inside its harness. Bolting retrieval onto an off-the-shelf small model is not enough, and often worse than nothing. In that setting, dense retrieval lowered exact match at every size tested, including 360M (−1.5 points in an n=200 pilot) ([2603.11513](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.11513)). Training changes the picture. TinyAgent-1.1B went from 12.71% to 78.89% on its 16-function task after SFT on 80K synthetic traces (~$500 of GPT-4-Turbo calls). A learned DeBERTa tool selector then lifted it to **80.06% against GPT-4-Turbo's 79.08%** while halving the prompt, but generic embedding tool-RAG dropped it to 74.88% ([TinyAgent](https://bair.berkeley.edu/blog/2024/05/29/tiny-agent/)). STAR took Qwen3-0.6B to BFCLv3 51.70, slightly above Llama-3.1-8B, while plain SFT *collapsed* its ACEBench score from 27.20 to 2.10 ([STAR](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.03022)). DGPO trained a Qwen2.5-0.5B search agent to 0.329 average EM against 0.353 for its 3B teacher. GRPO from a cold start "collapsed early" at that size, so the result needed a distillation warm start ([DGPO](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.20324)). Search-R1's ablation shows why tool output should be masked out of the loss: 0.431 with masking versus 0.343 without on 7B ([Search-R1](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.09516)). Planner-R1 adds that **at 8B, sparse rewards collapsed 3 of 5 runs and dense, per-step rewards were needed** to train stably ([Planner-R1](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.25779v1)); smaller models are unlikely to be more forgiving. Toolformer's self-supervised tool use only emerged at around 775M parameters ([Toolformer](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2302.04761)), while supervised and RL recipes reached it at 350M–600M ([MobileLLM](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.14905); [STAR](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.03022)). At 350M, then, the curriculum has to be explicit, because the model will not discover the behaviour on its own.

The harness side has its own measured levers. Query-aware selection and reordering can raise accuracy while removing three-quarters of the tokens: LongLLMLingua gained up to +21.4% on NaturalQuestions with ~4× fewer tokens and flattened the lost-in-the-middle curve ([LongLLMLingua](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2310.06839.md)). Running a separate compressor model next to a 500 MB model may cost as much as it saves, so a small reranker or BM25 is the proportionate version. Production agent builders converge on the same practices, with qualitative evidence only. They keep the stable prefix cached, mask tool logits rather than removing tools, recite the goal or todo list at the end of the context, keep errors visible, and treat context as an "attention budget" ([Manus](https://manus.im/en/blog/Context-Engineering-for-AI-Agents-Lessons-from-Building-Manus); [Anthropic](https://anthropic.com/engineering/effective-context-engineering-for-ai-agents)). Each maps onto the attention math. Recitation puts the unfilled goal in the high-attention recent window. Dedupe and trimming shrink n and remove near-duplicate distractors. Unique field labels and echoing the query key next to its value enlarge the logit gap. That last point is a theory-based prediction no paper has measured. Long, stable instructions can be "baked" into weights with a KL distillation objective, freeing context and reducing prompt forgetting ([Prompt Baking](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.13697)). This is unverified below 1B.

Latent interfaces give a consistent warning. Projected knowledge tokens (xRAG), trained KV caches (Cartridges) and knowledge tokens (KBLaM) save large amounts of compute and memory on fact lookup. They **lose on multi-hop**: xRAG scores 34.05 on HotpotQA against 38.79 for full-text RAG ([xRAG](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.13792)). None has been shown below 3B, and the Memory Decoder that does work at 0.5B is itself a second 0.5B model ([Memory Decoder](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.09874)). For your design, the bridge entity should come back as plain, labelled text, and compressed injection should be reserved for single-hop lookups if you use it at all.

Those results suggest a split of responsibilities. The harness owns knowledge (search, memory), exact computation (calculator, code execution), sequencing (a goal/slot state that does not accept an answer until required attributes are filled), and verification. The model owns deciding what to call next, reading the result, and formatting. The harness should also *teach* the model. It produces the traces, it supplies verifiable rewards, and it can gate when retrieval or asking the person is allowed. Self-RAG-style reflection tokens and SpeakRL-style clarification training show that "when to retrieve" and "when to ask" are learnable decisions. SpeakRL-7B reached 46.17% task success against 44.08% for Claude Sonnet 4 on MultiWOZ, but with a Sonnet judge and simulator and no penalty for over-asking ([SpeakRL](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.13159)).

## TypeScript: the harness can check code, but it cannot write it

Your numbers match the public record. Qwen2.5-Coder-0.5B-Instruct scores 61.6 on HumanEval but **2.0 on LiveCodeBench and 1.4 on BigCodeBench-Hard**. The 3B scores 10.8 and 14.2 ([Qwen2.5-Coder report](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.12186v3)). The 51% MultiPL-E TypeScript you measured belongs to the easy, likely-contaminated end of that range. Expert-level TypeScript means multi-file, type-level design and debugging, and nothing at ≤500 MB is close; the open models described as GPT-4o-comparable on code are around 32B ([Qwen blog](https://qwenlm.github.io/blog/qwen2.5-coder-family/)). The harness can contribute in three real ways. Retrieval of type definitions and API docs tells the model what exists (RepoCoder-style iterative repo retrieval improved completion by over 10% ([RepoCoder](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.12570))). A `tsc` and test loop catches errors and drives retries. Delegation hands hard problems to a bigger model. All three have a ceiling. Test-time search and verification beat a 14× larger model only on prompts where the small model already has non-trivial success; on the hardest problems, pretraining scale wins ([Snell et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.03314)). No small-model numbers exist for `tsc`-in-the-loop specifically. The defensible product shape is a local model that writes small, checkable TypeScript (glue code, edits, typed tool calls), verifies it with the compiler and tests, and escalates to Haiku/Sonnet-class models when verification keeps failing. Escalation should be a trained action, like `ask_user`.

## What is possible, what is not, and what is unproven

| Claim | Status | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| A ≤500 MB model plus harness can beat much larger models on narrow, fixed-tool, lookup-shaped tasks | **Possible, demonstrated at ~0.6–1.1B** | [TinyAgent](https://bair.berkeley.edu/blog/2024/05/29/tiny-agent/), [STAR](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.03022), [DGPO](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.20324) |
| Reliable 2-hop tool chaining at 350M–800M when the harness enforces hop steps and the model is trained with per-hop signal | **Possible in principle**; consistent with theory (hops as tokens) and small-model RL, but not shown on your task | [Sanford et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.09268), [2608.11233](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.11233) |

## Seven experiments that fit in $40 of Hugging Face Jobs

Every cost below is an estimate built from the parameter count and token volume. None of the cited papers report single-GPU wall-clock times at this scale, so treat each figure as a budget cap and stop runs that overshoot. Run them roughly in order, because Experiments 1–2 decide whether the later ones are worth their money. For every experiment, use a held-out evaluation with *unseen chain templates* (for example, train person→town→population and test company→HQ city→mayor) and *unseen hop counts* (train on 2–3, test on 4), and report strict accuracy on it.

**Experiment 1: diagnose the chaining failure (~$3, L4, ~3 h).** Take the fine-tuned LFM2.5-350M and off-the-shelf Qwen3.5-0.8B and run 200 failing held-out episodes. At the token where the model chooses its next action after the first tool result, record three things. First, the logit-lens rank of the bridge town's name across layers, following the Hopping Too Late protocol ([Biran et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12775)). Second, the attention mass from the attention layers onto the town tokens; for LFM2.5's convolution layers use plain forward hooks or nnsight. Third, the probability of the search tool versus the answer action. Label each failure as "didn't read the town", "read it but chose to answer", or "format error". Also run the same episodes with 0, 2, 4 and 8 irrelevant tool results added, to measure your own dilution curve. If most failures are "read it but answered", the fix is policy, and attention retrofits (Experiment 5) can be dropped.

**Experiment 2: harness-only fixes (~$2, L4, ~2 h, no training).** On the same checkpoints, A/B four changes, singly and combined. (a) A goal/slot recitation appended at the end of the context, for example "Still needed: population of <town>". (b) A state machine that blocks the `answer` action until every requested attribute has a sourced value. (c) Tool outputs reformatted as short `key: value` records that echo the queried entity, deduplicated and trimmed. (d) Constrained decoding that masks tool-name logits to the legal next actions. Theory predicts (a) and (c) increase the logit gap for the needed token. Change (b) is a guarantee rather than a learned behaviour, so it should move held-out strict accuracy on chaining failures immediately.

**Experiment 3: combinatorial SFT with masked tool output (~$6, A100, ~2 h for 3–4 short runs).** Regenerate the ~2,000 episodes as 6–10K across many template families. Use one hop per tool call, vary the ratio of composed to single-hop episodes, and use Haiku/Sonnet transcripts as teacher traces. Mask tool outputs out of the loss ([Search-R1](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.09516)). Compare 1:1 against 3:1 composed:atomic, because the grokking results say this ratio matters more than raw volume ([Wang et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.15071)). Train both LFM2.5-350M and Qwen3.5-0.8B; both fit ≤500 MB only when quantized (about 8-bit and 4-bit respectively). Watch for the STAR-style collapse, where plain SFT damages general tool use ([STAR](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.03022)). Keep a slice of general function-calling data in the mix.

**Experiment 4: GRPO with per-hop rewards on a mocked world (~$15, A100, ~6 h).** Start from the best Experiment 3 checkpoint, not the raw model. Build an offline environment: a small synthetic knowledge graph behind mocked `search`, `calculator` and `ask_user` tools, so rewards are exact and rollouts need no network. Use decomposed rewards in the style of [ToolRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.13958v1): valid format, partial credit for each required hop issued, correct final answer, and a penalty for answering before the required lookups. Avoid length rewards. Use LoRA. Switch to PPO if GRPO's reward curve collapses, which DGPO observed at 0.5B ([DGPO](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.20324)). Evaluate on the held-out templates and on 4-hop chains.

**Experiment 5: attention-math retrofit, only if Experiment 1 shows reading failures (~$6, A100, ~2.5 h).** Use Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct as the base, since it is a pure-attention model; LFM2.5 has only a few attention layers. Run three arms on identical Experiment 3 data with identical LoRA. Arm one is the control. Arm two adds a head-wise sigmoid output gate initialized near identity (bias ≈ +4) and trained alongside the LoRA. Arm three uses SSMax-style query scaling by s·log n with s initialized to 1/mean(log n), as in the paper's post-hoc setting ([SSMax](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.19399)). Measure the distractor curve from Experiment 1, attention entropy on tool-result tokens, and held-out strict accuracy. This is the first public test of a gate retrofit at this size, and a null result is likely and still informative. If the money is better spent elsewhere, the stretch alternative is the rank-16 recurrent-loop LoRA from [2608.11233](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.11233), which targets latent composition rather than reading.

**Experiment 6: does the MLP hold facts or skills? (~$2, L4, ~2 h).** On the Experiment 3 checkpoint, zero- or mean-ablate one MLP layer at a time, and also shrink MLPs by magnitude-pruning neurons at 10–30%. Measure two things: closed-book factual QA, and tool-use success when the same facts are supplied by the harness. If tool use holds up while recall falls, that is direct evidence that a harness-fed model can trade MLP fact capacity for skill capacity at this scale. If both fall together, the MLP is carrying skills you cannot spare.

**Experiment 7: what the compiler loop adds to TypeScript (~$3, L4, ~3 h).** On MultiPL-E TypeScript, compare pass@1 against pass after up to three rounds of `tsc` errors and failing-test output fed back to the model. Run Qwen2.5-Coder-0.5B-Instruct and Qwen3.5-0.8B. Also log the share of problems still failing after three rounds, which is the escalation rate a delegate-to-Haiku policy would face. This puts a number on the verifier's real contribution and on how often the small model would need to hand off.

These seven total about $37, leaving a small reserve for one re-run. If Experiment 2's state machine alone recovers most held-out chaining, move Experiment 5's money into Experiment 4, which is where generalization to new combinations is decided.

## Conclusion

Your question assumed that focusing a small model is mainly a property of its attention math. The evidence says focus is a budget shared between the model and its harness. Softmax gives each head a fixed amount of discriminative power per log-n of context, and depth gives each forward pass a fixed number of hops. A small model has less of both, so the pair wins by spending the harness's strengths on the model's weak points. The harness shrinks and labels the context so the needed key stands out, splits chains into single lookups, refuses to accept an answer with missing slots, and supplies the facts and arithmetic that a 1–2 bit-per-parameter MLP should not be memorizing. Attention changes such as gates and differential heads are worth adopting when you pretrain or distill your own model, mainly for robustness to distractors and for quantization. As retrofits onto a pretrained 350M model, they are speculative.

The less obvious implication is that most of the remaining gap is a training-distribution problem the harness can solve. Your 98% versus 27% split, the minimal surface transfer of the recurrence retrofit, and the collapse of plain SFT at 0.6B all say the same thing: small models learn the episode shapes they are shown. A harness that can generate unlimited, verifiable, combinatorially varied episodes and score each hop is the most valuable piece of this system. It turns the harness from a crutch into the model's teacher. That holds for chaining. It does not hold for expert TypeScript, where the honest design is a small local model that writes, compiles and checks, and knows when to hand off.
